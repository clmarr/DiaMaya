# DiaMaya

## Transcription conventions

### Final vowels

Writing Early Hieroglyphic Maya (HGM) as written, with vocalisms -- this will allow analyses that have been made in scholarship a chance to emerge from the data on their own without assuming them a priori. That way, we can test the various proposals as to synharmonic vs. disharmonic spellings (mostly Houston et al. 1998, 2004 vs. Lacadena & Wichmann 2004). 
In the 1950s, Knorozov demonstrated that the Maya used phonetic spelling (CV) alongside logographic (mostly CVC). So-called "synharmonic" spelling is when the last written syllable features the same vowel as the preceding one (e.g. CeC-Ce) whereas disharmonic spelling is when the vowels are different (e.g. CeC-Ca). There is no consensus on how to interpret this disharmonic spelling. Stephen Houston, David Stuart and John Robertson (1998, 2004) argue that disharmonic spelling encodes "complex", i.e. non-short vowels. These can take the shapes VV, V' or Vh in Hieroglyphic Maya. Lacadena & Wichmann (2004) on the other hand present more refined, complicated rules where certain vowel combinations encode specific types of complex vowels. It seems that most researchers today loosely follow Houston et al. because the suggestions by Lacadena & Wichmann do not entirely add up. Still, there must be some kind of pattern to it.

## Stages 
Based on the same data, scholars come to entirely opposite conclusions on what exact kind of Cholan HGM represents. Mora-Marín, Hopkins & Josserand (2009) find that the texts record the ancestor of all Cholan languages, effectively Proto-Cholan, and that the disintegration of this proto-language can be observed in the HG texts (see also Mora-Marín 2009). The most prominent view (e.g. Houston et al. 2000, Law et al. 2009) holds that HGM is only the ancestor of Eastern Cholan (Choltí and Ch'orti), not Western Cholan (Chol and Chontal). This view puts HGM - Choltí - Ch'orti' in a direct line of succession making Ch'orti' the only surviving daughter language of HGM and Choltí an intermediary step between them. Wichmann (2002) points out that Choltí and Ch'orti' are more likely to be sister languages than mother and daughter. Korobzow (2025) strongly argues against direct succession as well based on evidence from verbal morphology. Wichmann (2006) offers an intermediate view and describes a selection of both typically Eastern and typically Western features (also based on previous work, e.g. Lacadena & Wichmann 2002 & Hruby & Child 2004). 

Our basic assumptions (for now) loosely follow Wichmann. On naming conventions see Korobzow (2025: 52-53).

Proto-Cholan : ancestor of all the below.

Early Hieroglyphic Maya : circa 250 CE (the earliest texts are from circa 300 BCE but they are difficult to understand)

West, East Hieroglyphic Maya : later regionalized varieties of HGM (Lacadena & Wichmann 2002, Wichmann 2006)

Later texts may show conservative forms but earlier texts will not show a sound change that has not yet happened.

Chontal -- "Western Cholan", colonial attestation in Acalan Chontal (c. 1600) and modern attestation (Tabasco Chontal). 

Ch'orti' -- "Eastern Cholan", supposed descendant of East Hieroglyphic Maya (name attested in 1768 proves sound change PM *l > r, earliest actual data 1834, see Korobzow 2025: 41-59)

Choltí -- "Eastern Cholan" supposed descendant of East Hieroglyphic Maya, attested in one document from 1695 (Robertson, Law & Haertel 2010)

Chol (dialectally Ch'ol) -- "Western Cholan" but position to be examined
